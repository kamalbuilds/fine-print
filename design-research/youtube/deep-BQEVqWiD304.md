## Techniques, with timestamps

- **0:00 Alignment builds relationships.** Alignment is "how we can create order, structure and suggest relationships between visual elements".
- **2:33 Grid families.** Column, modular, hierarchical and baseline are the classics. Axial, diagonal and radial are the alternatives.
- **3:33 Column grid for type.** "especially practical when dealing with large amounts of type as a designer can flow the type from one column to the next and maintain a consistent paragraph width".
- **4:35 Modules and spatial zones.** "when multiple modules are assigned or grouped for a specific area of content, this is called a spatial zone".
- **5:37 Hierarchical grid.** Modules "[of] various proportions to outline order and priority and establish a clear primary hook or focal point".
- **7:11 Baseline grid.** "They create a vertical rhythm throughout the document and set a place to anchor type, so it's not placed arbitrarily."
- **8:13 Axial grid.** One axis line "will typically divide a composition into two distinct zones where text and image can then be placed or lined on either side". It is "good at drawing and leading your eye because it creates very high contrast".
- **11:50 Ask what grid a reference uses.** "when you look and observe other designs ask yourself what sort of grid has been used".

## What applies to Fine Print

- The diptych is an axial grid: one vertical axis per finding, with the claim on one side and the policy's words on the other. Make the axis visible (a hairline rule), because the axis is the argument: claim versus evidence.
- Put a baseline grid under both halves so the claim and quote rows line up across the axis.
- The page as a whole is a hierarchical grid. The product input and verdict form the hook zone, the six diptychs are equal modules, and the gateway panel is a narrow supporting zone. Do not give the panel the same visual weight as the findings.
